3. The nearby literature changes the novelty claim

  • Young et al. (2024), Phase equilibria of sub-Neptunes and super-Earths: establishes the planetary phase-equilibrium context.
  • Gilmore & Stixrude (2026), Nature 650, 60–64: first-principles simulations demonstrate H₂–MgSiO₃ miscibility and reactive chemistry. This is a candidate thermodynamic foundation, not a complete model for all of Hakim's elements.
  • Rogers, Young & Schlichting (2025): couples hydrogen–silicate miscibility to planetary structure and evolution. Its Gibbs mixing formulation is a practical starting point for computing coexistence and curvature; verify parameters against the final Nature paper.
  • Tejada Arevalo et al. (2026), Sub-Neptune Memories I: includes silicate–hydrogen phase separation and silicate rain in APPLE, with atmospheric silicate depletion and thermal evolution. The abstract reports acceptance to ApJ. This is especially relevant prior work for the proposed atmospheric effect.
  • Rogers & Schlichting (2026 preprint): explores miscibility with atmospheric escape. Marcum et al. (2026 preprint) provides a supercritical silicate–hydrogen EOS at a specified composition; a fixed-composition EOS alone does not determine the composition curvature needed for a spinodal.

These related studies are not identified here as Atmodeller applications. The potential contribution is a quantitatively consistent connection between separation and multicomponent atmospheric chemistry, plus a test of whether separation kinetics matters. “Phase separation affects atmospheres” is already represented in the literature.

4. What a spinodal extension actually requires

At fixed temperature and pressure, a binary homogeneous mixture is locally unstable when the curvature of its molar Gibbs free energy is negative:

g″(x; T,P) < 0.
At coexistence: g′(xα) = g′(xβ) = [g(xβ) − g(xα)] / (xβ − xα).
Bulk composition: x̄ = (1 − λ)xα + λxβ, with 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1.

The common tangent gives the binodal endpoints and the lever rule gives phase amounts. The spinodal marks the loss of local stability; between it and the binodal a homogeneous state is metastable and separation requires nucleation. Equal slopes alone are insufficient: the tangent intercepts must agree and the global lower convex envelope must be selected. In a reacting multicomponent system, use the constrained free-energy Hessian in independent composition coordinates, after specifying which reactions equilibrate on the perturbation timescale.

For the same free energy, budgets and constraints, full equilibrium does not remember whether separation happened through nucleation or spinodal decomposition. A specifically spinodal atmospheric effect requires finite timescales, transport, trapping, cooling, escape, or other departures from full equilibrium. Local demixing also does not automatically deliver material to the observable atmosphere.

My proposed kinetic stage would compare separation/coarsening, chemical equilibration, convective mixing, settling, cooling and escape times. Only then choose a reduced exchange model or a Cahn–Hilliard/advection model. A phase-field calculation needs mobility, gradient-energy and transport constraints; JAX differentiation does not supply those physical parameters.

engine.pySolves reaction, mass, activity and stability residuals. A converged root is not by itself proof of the globally stable state of a nonconvex mixture. Add phase-stability testing and branch selection.
solubility/core.py: Solubility.concentrationReturns concentration in ppmw. Useful for existing dissolution laws, but a single-valued solubility function alone cannot represent a miscibility gap, metastability and evolving phase fractions.

No spinodal, binodal, miscibility or Margules implementation was found by targeted searches of the checked-out package and tests. This is a source-inspection finding, not an exhaustive mathematical audit.

Main scientific integration risk: the H₂–MgSiO₃ phase model uses bulk thermodynamic components with chemical reactions already represented in its fitted free energy. Hakim's chemistry has explicit molecules and SiO₂-based mantle exchange. Those are different bases. Mapping them carelessly can double-count reactions, mismatch standard states, or violate Mg/Si/O balances. He/C/N effects on the phase diagram also need data or clearly bounded approximations. Do not simply add a demixing correction on top of all existing dissolution terms.

Keep internal units explicit: Atmodeller uses K and bar in these interfaces; published phase models may use GPa (1 GPa = 10,000 bar). Distinguish molecular H₂ abundance from hydrogen expressed as an equivalent component, and mole fractions from mass fractions.

6. A staged project with a useful stopping point

  1. Reproduce a baseline. Use the archived Hakim setup/version and a small representative subset; compare data, then port to v2 and quantify differences before adding new physics.
  2. Implement binary phase equilibrium independently. Reproduce a published H₂–MgSiO₃ binodal, calculate the spinodal from the same free energy, and verify common tangents, mass conservation, positive phase amounts and lower total Gibbs energy. Check the accessible pressure/temperature paths against crystallization and calibration limits.
  3. Establish a consistent chemistry coupling. Start at fixed P–T with explicit component mapping and shared elemental budgets. A one-way “separate then speciate” experiment can screen sensitivity, but must be labelled approximate until chemical-potential feedback is closed.
  4. Compare three controlled cases. Existing model; equilibrium demixing; finite-rate separation with transport. Hold total elemental budgets and the imposed physical history fixed. Track SiH₄/CH₄, H₂O/H₂, gas Si/C, mean molecular weight, and reservoir inventories. For ratios, also report absolute abundances so vanishing denominators do not create misleading signals.
  5. Test whether the effect survives. Propagate to upper-atmosphere chemistry with condensation/rainout and mixing/quenching assumptions. Explore degeneracies with metallicity, melt fraction and redox. Use oxygen mass conservation when studying redox feedback; an imposed oxygen buffer answers a different question.

Suggested first research question: “How robust is the silane–methane magma-ocean diagnostic to hydrogen–silicate demixing?” Add “spinodal kinetics” to the claim only if the finite-rate case differs materially from the equilibrium case under plausible physical timescales. A negligible effect within the credible parameter range would also be an informative result.

The most useful remaining context is the thesis's chemical system, pressure–temperature range and original Atmodeller version. The user has confirmed that all three separation pathways belong in scope.

7. Expanded scope: three separation pathways

Research objective: determine where each pathway is thermodynamically accessible, whether its kinetics allows separation before cooling or crystallization intervenes, and how the resulting reservoirs affect atmospheric composition. All three belong in the investigation; their simultaneous occurrence on one planet is a hypothesis to test.

PathwayEvidence and candidate atmospheric effectFirst model requirement
Hydrogen–silicateThe previously cited Gilmore–Stixrude and Rogers studies provide the starting point. Test changes in hydrogen storage, accessible silicon, and SiH₄/CH₄ under Hakim-like conditions.Validate the binary free-energy surface, then reconcile its component basis with the reacting chemistry.

A single fixed-composition MgSiO₃ or SiO₂ component cannot describe Fe-rich versus silica-rich melt separation.

The coupling is part of the problem. Hydrogen and water share H/O budgets and react through redox chemistry. Changes in melt composition affect volatile chemical potentials, and volatiles can move the melt immiscibility boundaries. Three independently fitted binary corrections do not uniquely determine a consistent multicomponent free energy. As an additional consistency check, Gupta, Stixrude & Schlichting's hydrogen–water study shows why ideal H₂–H₂O mixing cannot automatically be assumed throughout planetary interiors.

The proposed software structure is one phase-equilibrium framework with separate, explicitly calibrated thermodynamic models for each system. It should expose chemical potentials, constrained stability, coexistence compositions, phase amounts and calibration bounds. Validate each model in isolation; then combine compatible models using one conserved elemental inventory and consistent standard states. For the melt–melt branch, track composition-specific solubility and the exchange of both melts with the atmosphere. Include Fe oxidation states if testing redox feedback.

First map the three pathways over their justified composition–pressure–temperature domains, including competition with crystallization. Next measure atmospheric sensitivity for each pathway independently. Only then investigate overlapping regimes and kinetic feedbacks. I would still use hydrogen–silicate as the first implementation benchmark because of the direct Hakim connection, while reviewing water–silicate and melt–melt thermodynamic data from the start. This ordering keeps all three in scope.

Evidence boundary: published liquid immiscibility or volatile partitioning establishes neither negative free-energy curvature nor a spinodal rate. For every branch, distinguish equilibrium coexistence, evidence for spinodal decomposition, and predicted atmospheric consequences. This update changes the research design; no phase-separation implementation or new atmospheric prediction has been produced.
